NEIGHBORHOOD PASSPORT
Strategy, Marketing, Product, and Technical Implementation Document
Version 0.1 | Working strategy

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Neighborhood Passport is a local customer-development service for independent restaurants, bars, cafes, retailers, and other small businesses. It helps merchants fill slow periods and convert first-time visitors into repeat customers through coordinated offers, events, social discovery, physical collectibles, digital credentials, and measurable follow-up.

The product is not primarily a discount marketplace and it is not a generic social-media management service. It is a managed local loyalty network:

    Discover local business
        -> claim passport or join community
        -> visit during a targeted period
        -> receive a physical stamp and digital credential
        -> follow the local feed or RSVP to an event
        -> visit another participating business
        -> complete a route or milestone
        -> unlock status, reward, or exclusive access
        -> return and refer someone else

The central strategic thesis is:

    The first offer is not the product. It is the beginning of a relationship.

The agency operates the network, campaigns, creative, event promotion, customer journey, merchant reporting, and loyalty mechanics. Merchants receive incremental demand, customer capture, repeat-visit campaigns, and participation in a local network. Customers receive practical savings, memorable experiences, recognition, collectible artifacts, and a stronger connection to places they frequent.

Blockchain and Web3 components should be invisible or optional to ordinary customers. Use them where they add durable identity, portability, verifiable attendance, collectibility, or cross-business utility. Use ordinary web, Wallet passes, QR codes, and staff verification where those are simpler.

1. BUSINESS PROPOSITION

Working name: Neighborhood Passport

One-sentence proposition:

    Neighborhood Passport helps independent local businesses fill slow periods and turn first-time visitors into regulars through coordinated offers, events, collectible memberships, open social publishing, and measurable loyalty campaigns.

Customer problem:

- Social posting is inconsistent and difficult for owners to maintain.
- Paid advertising is hard to measure.
- Generic discounts can attract one-time bargain hunters.
- Customer data is fragmented across platforms.
- Businesses compete individually instead of benefiting from local network effects.
- Slow periods remain underutilized.
- Conventional loyalty programs are often forgettable and disconnected from community identity.

Product solution:

- Identify a merchant's slow but profitable periods.
- Build an offer that protects margin and encourages a desired behavior.
- Distribute it through local advertising, social content, events, partner businesses, QR/NFC objects, and Atmosphere channels.
- Capture consent and participation through a passport or digital pass.
- Reward the next action: return visit, event attendance, cross-business visit, or referral.
- Report verified participation, repeat behavior, and estimated economics.

Positioning:

    We turn unused local capacity into regular customers.

Alternative positioning:

    A local demand-generation and retention network for independent businesses.

2. TARGET MARKET AND INITIAL WEDGE

Start narrow. The initial target should be independent businesses within one geographically coherent Chicago neighborhood or commercial corridor.

Best early merchant candidates:

- Independent bars with slow weekday periods.
- Casual restaurants with unused off-peak capacity.
- Cafes and bakeries that want morning or afternoon traffic.
- New businesses trying to establish local regulars.
- Businesses with visually engaging products or events.
- Complementary merchants willing to cross-promote.

Avoid beginning with a broad promise to serve every small business. A narrow neighborhood makes the network visible, makes campaigns repeatable, and reduces travel and coordination costs.

Ideal pilot network:

- Three to five merchants.
- One neighborhood or walkable corridor.
- One shared campaign theme.
- One physical passport design.
- One or two recurring events.
- Fifty to one hundred initial passports.

3. MARKETING STRATEGY

3.1 Marketing objectives

Merchant-side objectives:

- Fill selected slow periods.
- Acquire measurable first-time visitors.
- Encourage a second visit within 14 to 30 days.
- Increase permission-based customer contacts.
- Generate referrals and cross-business visits.
- Reduce dependence on rented attention from large platforms.

Customer-side objectives:

- Make joining feel like belonging, not merely signing up for discounts.
- Make the next desired action obvious and easy.
- Make progress visible through stamps, status, and collectibles.
- Create reasons to visit multiple local businesses.
- Build a habit around events and slow-period rituals.

3.2 Customer acquisition channels

Offline:

- Passport distribution at participating merchants.
- Countertop NFC/QR plaques.
- Table tents and receipt inserts.
- Posters in nearby businesses.
- Hotel, apartment, office, and community partnerships.
- Event handouts and neighborhood markets.
- Physical collectible objects distributed after qualifying behavior.

Paid digital:

- Radius-targeted Meta, TikTok, Google, or other local ads.
- Separate landing pages and tracking links for each channel.
- Creative built around a specific slow-period action, not general awareness.
- Retargeting only where consent and applicable platform rules permit.

Organic social:

- Merchant posts on Bluesky and other social channels.
- A neighborhood account and curated local feed.
- Short-form video and photo content.
- Customer stories and event recaps.
- Merchant-to-merchant cross-posting.
- Starter Packs recommending participating merchants and local feeds.

Atmosphere distribution:

- Bluesky account for public announcements.
- Custom local feed such as “Tonight in [Neighborhood]” or “Passport Offers Nearby.”
- Bluesky Starter Pack recommending merchants, community accounts, and the local feed. Starter Packs can include recommended accounts and custom feeds and can be shared by link or QR code.[5]
- Smoke Signal/atmo.rsvp/OpenMeet for event discovery and RSVP. Smoke Signal currently indexes events and RSVPs published across the Atmosphere.[1]
- Standard.site-compatible publishing through tools such as Leaflet, pckt.blog, Offprint, or a custom publisher for merchant stories and long-form updates.[2][6]

3.3 Campaign design

Every campaign should have:

- A named customer behavior.
- A defined slow period or capacity objective.
- A customer-facing reason to act now.
- A trackable source.
- A controlled reward.
- A second-visit mechanism.
- A measurement plan.

Example: Tuesday Regulars

1. Local ad and merchant plaque promote a Tuesday offer.
2. Customer scans QR or taps NFC.
3. Customer claims a passport or digital pass.
4. Customer visits during the slow period.
5. Merchant provides a physical stamp and digital achievement.
6. Customer receives a return offer valid within 14 days.
7. Customer earns a referral reward after bringing a new guest.
8. Customer is invited to the next event.

Avoid permanent blanket discounts. Prefer offers that:

- Apply only during slow periods.
- Require a minimum spend.
- Promote higher-margin products.
- Encourage a second visit.
- Reward bringing a new customer.
- Reward cross-business routes.
- Create urgency without training customers to wait for discounts.

3.4 Network-effect campaigns

Neighborhood Passport becomes more valuable as more merchants participate.

Possible shared programs:

- Neighborhood Explorer: visit three different businesses.
- Off-Peak Champion: complete three slow-period visits.
- Event Collector: attend four neighborhood events.
- Local Builder: refer a new passport holder.
- Seasonal Route: complete a winter, spring, summer, or holiday route.
- Founding 100: first cohort receives numbered membership objects.

Each merchant benefits from the marketing activity of the others. A customer acquired by one business becomes a potential customer for the rest of the network.

3.5 Content strategy

Content should be organized around local behavior rather than generic posting volume.

Content pillars:

- What is happening nearby today?
- Why visit during this slow period?
- What is new at each merchant?
- What can customers collect or unlock?
- Which local route or challenge is active?
- Which customer or merchant story deserves attention?
- What event is coming next?

Recommended content production:

- One monthly photo/video session per merchant or group of nearby merchants.
- Reusable templates for offers, events, route progress, and reward unlocks.
- Merchant-specific pages and cross-business versions of each campaign.
- Weekly local feed curation.
- Monthly results post or private merchant report.

4. PRODUCT EXPERIENCE

4.1 The physical passport

The passport should be visually desirable before technology is explained.

Possible features:

- Specialty paper.
- Distinctive cover and neighborhood seal.
- Unique serial number.
- Space for merchant stamps.
- QR code fallback.
- Hidden NFC tag in the cover or membership card.
- Printed rules and first reward.
- Space for dates, event names, or short notes.

The physical stamp records the emotional experience. The digital record supports measurement, portability, and automation.

4.2 Merchant stamps

Each merchant receives a distinctive stamp representing its identity:

- Bakery: wheat or oven emblem.
- Bar: moon, glass, or neon sign.
- Bookstore: open book.
- Cafe: neighborhood bird or cup.
- Restaurant: chef's knife or signature ingredient.

Stamps can be issued for:

- Off-peak visits.
- Event attendance.
- Cross-business visits.
- Referrals.
- Seasonal challenges.
- Milestone completion.

4.3 Digital credentials and artifacts

Potential artifacts:

- First Visit card.
- Tuesday Regular badge.
- Founding Regular credential.
- Event attendance badge.
- Neighborhood Explorer achievement.
- Off-Peak Champion badge.
- Local Builder referral badge.
- Seasonal collection.

Crossmint-style walletless delivery is a suitable technical foundation for optional blockchain credentials because tokens can be delivered to an email-associated wallet and the platform advertises fiat billing, tokenization APIs, wallet support, updates, burns, and airdrops.[unverified from current source set; verify vendor terms before procurement]

POAP-style campaigns are suitable for event memories and attendance-based rewards. They should be treated as event credentials, not as the entire loyalty system.

4.4 Wallet passes

Apple Wallet and Google Wallet provide the practical everyday layer.

Apple supports loyalty and membership passes distributed through QR codes, NFC tags, SMS, email, and apps. Passes can update points, balances, offers, and seasonal campaigns, and can provide notifications.[unverified from current source set; see Apple source in research notes]

Google Wallet supports issuing loyalty passes across Android apps, websites, email, and SMS through API-based flows.[unverified from current source set; see Google source in research notes]

Recommended separation:

- Wallet pass: current utility, offer, status, barcode, and reminders.
- Blockchain credential: collectible achievement, event memory, or portable status.
- Physical passport: ritual, visible progress, and social object.

5. SOFTWARE SOLUTIONS

5.1 Pilot software stack

Customer-facing:

- Mobile-first passport webpage.
- Merchant pages.
- Event pages.
- QR and NFC entry points.
- Apple Wallet/Google Wallet pass where practical.
- Optional Crossmint credential.
- Optional POAP-style event badge.

Merchant-facing:

- Staff verification page.
- Campaign and offer editor.
- Stamp issuance log.
- Event and route manager.
- Customer permission and consent records.
- Simple dashboard.

Operations:

- SQLite, Airtable, or Google Sheets for the pilot.
- UTM links and source-specific QR codes.
- Email/SMS automation with consent management.
- Static or server-rendered webpages.
- Analytics with campaign, merchant, and period dimensions.

Do not build a native mobile app, full POS integration, cryptocurrency, or custom social network before behavior and willingness to pay are proven.

5.2 Atmosphere software layer

Use existing Atmosphere products first:

- Bluesky: neighborhood announcements and merchant content.
- Custom feed: curated local offers, events, and merchant posts. AT Protocol custom feeds can provide independent algorithms to compatible clients.[4]
- Starter Pack: onboarding to merchants, feeds, and neighborhood accounts.[5]
- Smoke Signal/atmo.rsvp/OpenMeet: event discovery, creation, and RSVP.[1]
- Standard.site-compatible publishing: business stories, guides, newsletters, and recurring updates.[2][6]
- Blento or similar page tools: rapid prototyping of Atmospheric profile pages.[2][6]

Longer-term owned layer:

Define an open Neighborhood Passport Lexicon for public records such as:

- merchant
- offer
- event
- stamp
- route
- achievement
- reward
- neighborhood

Public records can power merchant pages, a neighborhood map, event discovery, and custom feeds. Private customer details and redemption history should remain in the agency's private operational system unless explicitly consented to.

AT Protocol is a federated social-data protocol, not a payment blockchain. Its signed repositories, DIDs, records, feeds, and App Views are appropriate for public identity, publishing, discovery, and interoperability. It should not replace the private loyalty ledger or payment processor.

- Passport.
- Stamp.
- Event.
- RSVP.
- Credential.

Minimum event fields:

- Consent status.

5.4 Core metrics

Primary:

- Verified repeat behavior per participating customer.
- Incremental off-peak visits generated per merchant dollar.

Secondary:

- Passport enrollments.
- QR scans.
- NFC taps.
- Stamp issuance.
- Credential claims.
- Event RSVPs.
- Offer redemption rate.
- Second visit within 14 days.
- Second visit within 30 days.
- Cross-business visits.
- Referral visits.
- Average transaction value.
- Estimated contribution margin.
- Staff time required.
- Customer-list growth with valid consent.

Do not use follower count, impressions, or NFT claims as the primary success measures.

6. HARDWARE AND PHYSICAL SOLUTIONS

6.1 Basic NFC

Use inexpensive NFC cards, stickers, plaques, and tokens for convenience and discovery. NTAG213/215/216 chips are designed for consumer applications including smart advertising, retail, authentication, and mobile companion tags.[unverified from current source set; see NXP research note]

Likely objects:

- Passport cover tag.
- Merchant countertop plaque.
- NFC membership card.
- Wooden or acrylic collectible token.
- Event badge.
- Sticker or packaging tag.
- Keychain or bottle token.

Each tag should normally contain a resolvable URL, not private customer information:

    https://passport.example/tap/AB7K29

The server should apply the rules and record the event.

Use QR codes as the universal fallback.

6.2 Secure NFC for premium artifacts

For founding memberships, scarce rewards, or higher-value access, investigate NXP NTAG 424 DNA. It supports AES-128 cryptography and Secure Unique NFC messages that produce authenticated, tap-unique data. The phone reads the changing URL, the server verifies the authentication, and the system can confirm that a genuine tag was present.[unverified from current source set; see NXP NTAG 424 DNA research note]

Use secure NFC only when the reward justifies the additional cost and implementation complexity. A basic NFC tag is sufficient for ordinary campaign discovery.

6.3 Physical collectible objects

Possible objects:

- Numbered metal founding cards.
- Wooden merchant tokens.
- Ceramic event tiles.
- Enamel pins.
- Custom bottle-cap tokens.
- Stamped paper cards.
- Acrylic badges.
- Seasonal passport inserts.
- Small display plaques.

The object should have real emotional or social utility. Do not imply that it is an investment or that it will appreciate financially. Its value is access, recognition, memory, and belonging.

6.4 Staff equipment

Pilot equipment:

- Staff smartphone.
- QR/NFC merchant plaque.
- Stamp and ink pad.
- Passport supply.
- Optional small label printer.
- Optional tablet for events.
- Web-based verifier.

Avoid dedicated readers, vending machines, POS modifications, and automated hardware in the first pilot.

8. 90-DAY WORKSTREAM

Workstream 1: Merchant validation, days 1-15

Tasks:

- Select one Chicago neighborhood.
- Interview at least 10 independent businesses.
- Identify slow but profitable periods.
- Ask how repeat visits are currently measured.
- Identify three to five pilot merchants.
- Agree on campaign rules and data handling.

Gate:

- At least three merchants express concrete interest.
- At least two agree to run a pilot.
- Each pilot merchant identifies a measurable slow-period problem.

Workstream 2: Customer behavior design, days 10-25

Tasks:

- Choose the first route or campaign.
- Define the first five qualifying actions.
- Design reward economics.
- Design physical stamps and artifacts.
- Define anti-abuse rules.
- Decide what is public and private.

Gate:

A stranger understands how to join, earn a stamp, and reach the first reward in under one minute.

Workstream 3: Physical prototype, days 20-40

Tasks:

- Design passport paper and cover.
- Produce three to five merchant stamps.
- Add QR codes and one NFC tag.
- Produce 50-100 passports.
- Test scanning, stamping, and mobile usability.

Gate:

The passport is attractive, understandable, and operationally easy for staff.

Workstream 4: Atmosphere distribution, days 25-45

Tasks:

- Create neighborhood Bluesky identity.
- Create or connect merchant accounts.
- Build a Starter Pack.
- Publish a custom local feed.
- Publish merchant stories.
- Create the first event series through Smoke Signal or a compatible event tool.

Gate:

A customer scanning the passport can reach the neighborhood page, events, participating businesses, local feed, and first reward within a few taps.

Workstream 5: Loyalty and credential system, days 35-60

Tasks:

- Create the first digital membership credential.
- Create the first event credential.
- Add Wallet pass if practical.
- Create second-visit trigger.
- Add referral milestone.
- Track redemptions and repeat visits.

Gate:

At least one campaign produces a measurable second action: return visit, event RSVP, cross-business visit, or referral.

Workstream 6: Live pilot, days 46-75

Tasks:

- Distribute passports.
- Run one campaign per merchant.
- Run one shared event.
- Train staff.
- Monitor customer and staff friction weekly.
- Record all redemptions and repeat behavior.

Workstream 7: Evaluation, days 76-90

Tasks:

- Calculate acquisition and repeat metrics.
- Estimate incremental off-peak contribution.
- Identify fraud and operational friction.
- Interview merchants, staff, and customers.
- Determine which rewards are valued.
- Decide whether to expand, redesign, or stop.

9. PILOT DESIGN

Pilot name: The Local 100 or [Neighborhood] Passport: Founding Route

Participants:

- Three to five businesses.
- Fifty to one hundred initial passport holders.
- One neighborhood.
- One shared route.

Customer experience:

1. Customer receives a physical passport.
2. Customer scans the QR code or taps NFC.
3. Customer views participating businesses and current offers.
4. Customer joins the first campaign.
5. Customer visits a merchant during a slow period.
6. Merchant stamps the passport.
7. Digital achievement is issued.
8. Customer is invited to the next event or return offer.
9. Customer visits a second merchant.
10. Customer completes a route and unlocks a physical or digital milestone.

Suggested first reward ladder:

- First visit: passport welcome stamp.
- Second visit: returning guest badge.
- Third visit at a different merchant: Neighborhood Explorer badge.
- Slow-period completion: Off-Peak Champion badge.
- Referral: Community Builder badge.
- Full route: numbered founding object or invitation-only event.

10. RISKS AND CONTROLS

Risk: Customers see the product as gimmicky crypto.

Control: Lead with local membership, events, savings, and collectible experiences. Keep blockchain behind the scenes.

Risk: Discounts destroy merchant margin.

Control: Restrict time windows, require minimum spend, promote high-margin items, and measure estimated contribution margin.

Risk: Staff do not use the system.

Control: Keep verification to one tap or scan. Use paper stamps as the primary ritual and digital verification as a simple secondary action.

Risk: Fraud or copied QR codes.

Control: Use server-side one-time redemption, rate limits, staff verification, and secure NFC only for valuable artifacts.

Risk: Customer privacy problems.

Control: Keep phone, email, purchase, and exact visit history private by default. Publish only opt-in achievements and aggregate activity.

Risk: Dependence on an early-stage Atmosphere application.

Control: Own the merchant database, domain, campaign rules, and content exports. Use Atmosphere products as distribution surfaces, not the sole system of record.

Risk: Building too much technology before proving demand.

Control: Start with physical passports, QR, basic NFC, mobile webpages, existing social/event tools, and a simple private database.

Risk: Merchants do not pay.

Control: Validate pricing before building a sophisticated App View. Sell the economic outcome: slow-period demand and repeat behavior.

12. FINAL STRATEGIC STATEMENT

Neighborhood Passport is a local customer-development network that combines:

- Targeted slow-period marketing.
- Physical passports and special merchant stamps.
- Digital Wallet passes.
- Optional blockchain-backed collectibles.
- Event attendance credentials.
- NFC and QR physical-digital bridges.
- Atmosphere publishing and social discovery.
- Custom local feeds and Starter Packs.
- Cross-business routes and rewards.
- Private measurement of repeat customer economics.

The signature product is not a token, a stamp, a webpage, or a social account in isolation. It is the system formed by their interaction:

    Physical passport creates ritual.
    Stamp creates memory.
    NFC/QR creates the bridge.
    Wallet pass creates convenience.
    Digital credential creates collectibility.
    Atmosphere creates open social distribution.
    Merchant network creates cross-business value.
    Measurement creates economic credibility.

The initial objective is not to build a new social network or payment blockchain. It is to prove that a coordinated physical-digital neighborhood identity can increase off-peak visits, repeat behavior, event participation, and referrals for independent businesses.
